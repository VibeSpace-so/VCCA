# Product Course: From "Is this worth building?" to "Is it growing?"

This course teaches vibe coders how to find, deliver, and grow product value. It is organized as a walkable graph of concepts, starting with customer discovery and problem validation, moving through research, metrics, onboarding, prioritization, experimentation, and growth.

## Learning path at a glance

### Is this worth building?

- **customer-discovery**: What would you learn if you spent a month listening before writing the first line of code?
- **assumption-mapping**: What beliefs are buried in your business model that nobody has tested yet?
- **problem-validation**: How do you prove the problem you want to solve is real and painful before designing a solution?
- **customer-segment**: Who exactly feels the problem so strongly that they will change their behavior to solve it?
- **discovery-interview**: What could you learn from a 30-minute conversation that no analytics dashboard could ever tell you?
- **jobs-to-be-done**: What progress is your customer hiring your product to make in their life?
- **problem-interview**: How do you rank which customer pain matters most before you design a solution?
- **solution-interview**: How do you test a proposed solution before writing the code to build it?
- **beachhead-market**: What is the smallest market you can dominate before trying to serve everyone?
- **value-proposition**: Why should your target customer stop doing what they already do and choose you?
- **mom-test**: What questions would still give you useful answers if you asked your mother about your product?
- **earlyvangelist**: Who loves your unfinished product enough to shape it with you?
- **riskiest-assumption**: If only one of your beliefs about this idea could be wrong, which one would kill it?
- **minimum-viable-product**: What is the smallest thing you can put in front of customers that still teaches you something real?
- **concierge-mvp**: Can you deliver the value of your product by hand before you build the software?
- **fake-door-test**: What if you could measure real demand for a feature before building it?
- **landing-page-experiment**: Can a single page tell you whether anyone will pay for something that does not exist yet?

### How do I learn from customers?

- **qualitative-vs-quantitative-research**: When should you interview five people and when should you survey five hundred?
- **user-persona**: Who are you really designing for, and how do you keep them in the room?
- **usability-testing**: How do you find the problems in your design before you ship them to thousands of users?
- **survey-design**: How do you ask questions that produce reliable answers instead of biased noise?
- **customer-journey-map**: What does your product actually feel like from the customer's point of view?
- **product-analytics**: How do you know what users actually do inside your product once they sign up?
- **funnel-analysis**: Where are users dropping out of the path to value?
- **feedback-loop**: How do you turn customer input into product decisions before it goes stale?
- **customer-feedback-triage**: How do you turn a flood of feedback into a small set of actionable opportunities?
- **continuous-discovery**: How do you keep learning from customers without turning every decision into a research project?
- **opportunity-solution-tree**: How do you show which customer problems connect to which business outcomes and which possible solutions?

### What am I building toward?

- **product-vision**: What future are you trying to create, and how does it guide day-to-day decisions?
- **product-strategy**: How will the product win in its chosen market?
- **product-roadmap**: What is a roadmap for if it is not just a list of release dates?
- **now-next-later**: How do you communicate direction without committing to fake delivery dates?
- **okrs**: How do you set goals that are ambitious enough to stretch the team but measurable enough to grade?
- **outcome-vs-output**: Are you shipping features or changing user behavior?
- **leading-lagging-indicators**: How can you tell whether you are on track to hit next quarter's goals before it is too late to act?

### How do I decide what to build?

- **feature-prioritization**: How do you decide which feature is worth building when every stakeholder thinks theirs is the most important?
- **value-vs-effort**: What is the fastest way to separate quick wins from expensive bets?
- **rice-framework**: How can you compare very different ideas with a single number without hiding the uncertainty in the estimate?
- **kano-model**: How do you know which features will delight users and which will just prevent complaints?

### Are people sticking?

- **product-market-fit-signals**: What observable signals would you trust more than a founder's gut feeling to know people actually want your product?
- **the-40-percent-rule**: How disappointed would your users have to be before you can confidently say you have product-market fit?
- **superhuman-pmf-survey**: How can a four-question survey become your product roadmap?
- **net-promoter-score**: If someone asked your users whether they would recommend you, would the answers predict growth?
- **churn**: What if the users leaving are not the wrong fit, but the product is failing them at a predictable moment?
- **retention-curve**: How does your user base actually decay after the first day, and where does it flatten?
- **cohort-retention**: Are you measuring true retention, or just adding new users on top of old churn?
- **d1-d7-d30-retention**: Do your users come back the next day, the next week, and the next month?
- **pirate-metrics**: Are you measuring the whole user lifecycle, or only the parts that make you feel good?

### How do users get to value?

- **activation-funnel**: Where in the first session do users stop before they ever feel value?
- **aha-moment**: What single in-product action makes a user go from curious to committed?
- **time-to-value**: How much time does a new user have before they decide your product is not worth it?
- **first-user-experience**: What does someone see, feel, and do in the first sixty seconds after signing up?
- **empty-state-design**: What does your product look like before the user has done anything—and does it feel broken?
- **onboarding**: What is the shortest path from signup to first value, and why are you making it longer?
- **progressive-onboarding**: Do you really need to ask for everything up front, or can you request it exactly when it becomes useful?
- **activation-rate**: Of the people who sign up, how many actually do the thing that makes them want to stay?
- **engagement-loop**: What makes a user want to open your product again tomorrow without you having to remind them?
- **product-qualified-lead**: How do you know a free user is ready to buy without asking them?

### How do I grow it?

- **beta-program**: How do you launch to a small group and graduate with real evidence?
- **product-launch**: What makes a product launch more than a single announcement post?
- **experiment-design**: What is the written contract that makes an A/B test answer the right business question?
- **statistical-significance**: How unusual would your results be if the change actually had no effect?
- **control-group**: What does "normal" look like while you test a change?
- **minimum-detectable-effect**: What is the smallest lift your experiment is powered to detect?
- **sample-size**: How many users do you need before you can trust the result?
- **multivariate-testing**: How do you know which combination of changes is best?
- **willingness-to-pay**: What range of prices will customers actually accept?
- **pricing-experiment**: How do you find the price that maximizes value without guessing?
- **freemium**: How do you acquire millions of users when only a small fraction will pay?
- **product-led-growth**: What if the product itself, not sales, is your main growth channel?
- **bottom-up-saas**: How does a product spread from employees to the enterprise?
- **self-serve-onboarding**: How do you get a new user to value without a human handoff?
- **feature-adoption**: How do you get users to actually use the features you shipped?
- **network-effects**: Why does the product get better for every user who joins?
- **viral-loop**: How do existing users bring in new users as a natural byproduct of using the product?
- **growth-loop**: What is the self-reinforcing system that drives sustainable growth?

## Rationale

1. **Discovery and validation** come first because the most expensive mistake in product is building something no one needs. These concepts emphasize behavior over opinions and small tests over big launches.
2. **Customer research and personas** teach how to talk to users without being misled and how to synthesize research into concrete artifacts.
3. **Strategy and goals** provide the bridge between a long-term vision and the day-to-day choices a team makes.
4. **Prioritization** becomes necessary once the problem and strategy are clear and the backlog fills up faster than the team can ship.
5. **Product-market fit and retention metrics** tell a team whether the product is working before it invests in growth.
6. **Onboarding and activation** are the highest-leverage surfaces for turning signups into retained users.
7. **Experiments, pricing, and growth loops** scale the product once it is worth scaling.

## Detailed lessons

### customer-discovery

**Question:** What would you learn if you spent a month listening before writing the first line of code?

**Explanation:** Customer discovery is the disciplined search for your first customers and a deep understanding of their problems. It replaces slide-deck guesses with evidence gathered from the building by founders, not consultants.

**Apply:** Spend 2-4 weeks doing 15-20 unscripted conversations with people in your suspected target segment; focus on workflow and pain, not pitching.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders can build an app in a weekend, but they can also burn weeks on a product no one asked for; customer discovery prevents the elegant-solution-to-no-problem trap by forcing you to listen before you ship.

**Common misconception:** Discovery is a research phase you do once. Discovery is continuous; as the product changes, so does the customer profile.

**Example:** A B2B founder talks to 20 CFOs and learns they all manually email monthly board reports, not because of missing software, but because every board member wants a different format.

**Case study:** Omnea's founder, Ben Freeman, ran ~300 customer interviews with procurement leaders before writing code. The team identified that procurement tools were clunky and incomplete, which led them to build a two-way data orchestration layer instead of a simple dashboard. Omnea later raised a $50M Series B and counts Spotify, Wise, and MongoDB as customers.

**Resources:** https://steveblank.com/2009/09/17/the-path-of-warriors-and-winners/, https://web.stanford.edu/group/e145/cgi-bin/winter/drupal/upload/handouts/Four_Steps.pdf

**Next:** assumption-mapping, discovery-interview

### assumption-mapping

**Question:** What beliefs are buried in your business model that nobody has tested yet?

**Explanation:** Assumption mapping is a team exercise that makes explicit the desirability, feasibility, viability, and adaptability beliefs behind an idea, then plots them by importance and evidence. The "important + no evidence" quadrant becomes your immediate test queue.

**Apply:** In a 90-minute workshop, list 15-20 assumptions, tag them D/F/V/A, and place them on a 2x2 of importance vs. evidence.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders chase the next shiny feature; assumption mapping surfaces the beliefs that can kill the idea before you code them into your weekend build.

**Common misconception:** We have a lot of assumptions; we need to test all of them. Only the important, unproven ones matter; the rest can be documented risk.

**Example:** A team maps assumptions and realizes "users will pay $9/month" has zero evidence, while "we can use Stripe" has strong evidence. They test pricing first.

**Case study:** Webvan raised $800M and built highly automated warehouses but failed partly because it did not adequately test whether consumers valued 30-minute grocery delivery at a price that covered costs. Webvan is a cautionary case for skipping assumption mapping on the demand side.

**Resources:** https://www.strategyzer.com/library/how-assumptions-mapping-can-focus-your-teams-on-running-experiments-that-matter?xgtab=, https://roadmap.one/blog/posts/blog44-5-assumption-mapping/

**Prereqs:** customer-discovery
**Next:** problem-validation, riskiest-assumption

### problem-validation

**Question:** How do you prove the problem you want to solve is real and painful before designing a solution?

**Explanation:** Problem validation is the process of confirming that a specific audience feels a specific pain strongly and frequently enough to act on. It separates real demand from founder intuition by collecting concrete evidence about current behavior, workarounds, and willingness to switch.

**Apply:** Run 5-10 exploratory interviews asking about the last time the problem occurred and what the person did to solve it before you build any prototype.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders often take complaints as demand; problem validation stops you from building a polished tool people say they want but never actually use.

**Common misconception:** If people say the problem is annoying, they will buy a product for it. People overstate future behavior; only past behavior predicts future action.

**Example answer:** Problem validation is proving that a defined customer segment has an urgent, recurring problem they are already spending time or money to solve.

**Example:** A team wants to build a better invoice generator. Problem validation succeeds when 8/10 freelancers describe manually fixing formatting errors in Word before sending invoices.

**Case study:** StatementSync validated that freelance bookkeepers spent 10+ hours weekly manually extracting PDF bank transactions and already paid competitors $0.25-$1.00 per file. The founder scored problem severity 8/10, persona clarity 9/10, and willingness to pay 8/10 before writing code, then shipped an MVP in one week.

**Resources:** https://ashmaurya.com/blog/find-better-problems-worth-solving-with-the-customer-forces-canvas, https://dev.to/chudi_nnorukam/from-pain-point-to-mvp-i-built-statementsync-in-one-week-4ejh

**Prereqs:** assumption-mapping, customer-discovery
**Next:** customer-segment, riskiest-assumption

### customer-segment

**Question:** Who exactly feels the problem so strongly that they will change their behavior to solve it?

**Explanation:** Customer segment is a precisely defined group of people or organizations that share the problem, the urgency, and the ability to pay. Demographics alone are not enough; triggers, behavior, and motivation matter more.

**Apply:** Create a one-page segment profile with job title, trigger, current workaround, budget source, and where you can reach them.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders want broad appeal; a narrow segment lets you build one thing a specific group loves instead of a generic product no one pays for.

**Common misconception:** Our segment is "small businesses" or "millennials." These are categories, not segments. A real segment has a specific, urgent, shared problem.

**Example:** "Solo freelance bookkeepers in the U.S. who process 50+ PDF bank statements per month and currently pay per-file tools" is a segment; "small businesses" is not.

**Case study:** TripleTen, an edtech startup, had a high-volume lead funnel but low conversion. A simple entry-point survey and segmentation revealed that "motivation to learn" and "prior coding experience" were the strongest predictors of graduating. The product and sales teams used this to redesign the funnel and build targeted solutions.

**Resources:** https://annakhakho.substack.com/p/entry-point-segmentation-learning, https://steveblank.com/2009/11/30/customer-development-is-not-a-focus-group/

**Prereqs:** problem-validation, customer-discovery
**Next:** discovery-interview, value-proposition

### discovery-interview

**Question:** What could you learn from a 30-minute conversation that no analytics dashboard could ever tell you?

**Explanation:** A discovery interview is an open-ended, qualitative conversation designed to understand customer context, mental models, and unmet needs. It is not a sales call or a feature-preference poll; it is an investigation.

**Apply:** Draft a 5-question guide, then run 5 interviews where 80% of the time is spent on the interviewee, not your product.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders run on intuition and quick tests; a real discovery interview reveals the mental model behind behavior, not just surface opinions.

**Common misconception:** Good discovery interviews validate your idea. They should test and often refute your assumptions, not confirm them.

**Example:** A founder building course software learns that instructors do not need better video hosting; they need help collecting proof-of-completion for employers.

**Case study:** A 12-week discovery project at Laserhub focused on why metal-industry customers would switch from long-standing supplier relationships. 15 remote interviews with business owners, general managers, and procurement managers identified switching criteria and built a JTBD-based customer journey map. The result was a clear set of business objectives, including a 15% order-volume increase target.

**Resources:** https://ashmaurya.com/blog/how-to-systematically-uncover-big-problems-worth-solving, https://davidwenk.de/work/laserhub/

**Prereqs:** customer-segment, customer-discovery
**Next:** jobs-to-be-done, problem-interview

### jobs-to-be-done

**Question:** What progress is your customer hiring your product to make in their life?

**Explanation:** Jobs-to-be-done (JTBD) is a lens that treats customers as people trying to make progress under constraints, not as demographic buckets. You win by understanding the struggling moment that causes them to switch to a new solution.

**Apply:** Interview 8-10 recent switchers: ask what they used before, what triggered the switch, and what progress they were trying to make.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders copy features from competitors; jobs-to-be-done shows the real struggle users hire products for, so you can beat incumbents without matching every feature.

**Common misconception:** A job is a task in your product. The job is the life outcome the customer wants; your feature is just one candidate tool.

**Example:** A fast-food chain sold more milkshakes by discovering the morning customer hired them for a long, boring commute, not dessert, and made them thicker to last longer.

**Case study:** Intercom plateaued after positioning itself as an all-in-one customer communications platform. JTBD research by The Re-Wired Group revealed different teams bought it for different reasons (helpdesk vs. CRM). Intercom re-architected messaging and go-to-market around the specific jobs and achieved 500% growth in 18 months.

**Resources:** https://therewiredgroup.com/case-studies/intercom/, https://therewiredgroup.com/case-studies/milkshakes/

**Prereqs:** discovery-interview, customer-discovery
**Next:** problem-interview, value-proposition

### problem-interview

**Question:** How do you rank which customer pain matters most before you design a solution?

**Explanation:** A problem interview is a structured conversation that tests your top problem hypotheses against a specific customer segment. Its goal is to understand the customer's worldview, existing alternatives, and the severity of each problem.

**Apply:** Write a script with three hypothesized problems and ask 10 prospects to rank them by pain and describe how they currently solve them.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders pitch features and hear praise; ranking problems first stops you from building the founder's favorite feature instead of the customer's most painful one.

**Common misconception:** You should ask what features they want. The conversation is about problems and current workarounds, not your potential solution.

**Example:** A founder lists "slow reports," "manual data entry," and "no audit trail." Interviewees all say manual data entry is daily, but the others are quarterly; now you know where to start.

**Case study:** Marmelab's Lean Startup Adventure used Ash Maurya's Problem Interview script with CloudScreener. The interview revealed that the phrase "data administration" did not resonate with users, even those who used admin tools daily, and that the startup's terminology was "just plain wrong." The early insight prevented them from building around an internal vocabulary.

**Resources:** https://ashmaurya.com/blog/find-better-problems-worth-solving-with-the-customer-forces-canvas, https://marmelab.com/blog/2016/01/27/lean-startup-designing-a-problem-interview.html

**Prereqs:** jobs-to-be-done, discovery-interview
**Next:** solution-interview

### solution-interview

**Question:** How do you test a proposed solution before writing the code to build it?

**Explanation:** A solution interview is a structured conversation in which you walk a qualified prospect through a low-fidelity proxy for your product—a sketch, mockup, or prototype—and watch for what works, what is missing, and what they would pay. It is the second half of customer development: problem interviews confirm the pain, solution interviews confirm that your answer fits. The goal is learning, not selling, so the strongest signal is behavioral commitment, not polite praise.

**Apply:** After 10 problem interviews, recruit 5-10 prospects with the pain, show them a rough prototype or clickable mockup, ask them to walk through how they would use it, and end with a commitment ask such as an email, intro, or small deposit.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders often start coding the moment a problem is confirmed; a solution interview catches mismatches between the pain and your proposed answer before you sink engineering time.

**Common misconception:** A good solution interview confirms your idea. It should be designed to surface objections and falsify your hypothesis; compliments and head nods are the most dangerous data.

**Example:** A founder shows a B2B finance tool mockup to three accounting managers and notices they all ask how to export to Excel; that single question rewrites the first-release feature list.

**Case study:** Steve Blank's customer development model separates problem interviews from solution interviews; teams that skip the solution stage often build products that solve a real pain in the wrong way.

**Resources:** https://kromatic.com/real-startup-book/3-generative-product-research/solution-interview/, https://www.koji.so/docs/solution-interviews-guide

**Prereqs:** problem-interview, discovery-interview
**Next:** beachhead-market

### beachhead-market

**Question:** What is the smallest market you can dominate before trying to serve everyone?

**Explanation:** A beachhead market is the narrow, winnable customer segment you target first to gain a strong foothold before expanding. It must be small enough to focus your limited resources and large enough to support a business.

**Apply:** List 5 candidate segments, score each on urgency, reachability, budget, and adjacency to future markets, then pick one for the next 90 days.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders want to launch to everyone; a beachhead market lets you dominate a small niche and get real proof before scaling up.

**Common misconception:** A beachhead market is just the first market you find. It is chosen intentionally because you can win it quickly, not because it is convenient.

**Example:** A sports analytics startup could chase any sport, but chooses padel courts in Rotterdam because courts are willing to install cameras and the player community is tight-knit.

**Case study:** Facebook launched exclusively at Harvard, saturated the campus, then expanded to other universities and eventually the world. The tight community made viral growth possible before the product had to work for a broad, diverse audience.

**Resources:** https://www.pmftracker.com/frameworks/beachhead-market, https://www.doctormarket.fit/p/how-to-validate-your-beachhead-market

**Prereqs:** problem-interview, customer-segment, solution-interview
**Next:** value-proposition, smoke-test

### value-proposition

**Question:** Why should your target customer stop doing what they already do and choose you?

**Explanation:** A value proposition is the clear promise of the concrete outcome a customer gains from your product. It is not a slogan; it is a testable claim about how you relieve pain and create gain compared to current alternatives.

**Apply:** Write one sentence: "We help [segment] who [struggle] to [outcome] by [mechanism], unlike [current alternative]." Test it on a landing page.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders build cool things but struggle to explain why someone should care; a clear value proposition gives your landing page a single, testable reason to exist.

**Common misconception:** A value proposition is a clever tagline. If it does not name the customer, the problem, and the outcome, it is not a value proposition.

**Example answer:** A value proposition is a clear statement of the outcome you create for a specific customer and why it is better than their current alternative.

**Example:** "Hide sweat, not confidence" became a more compelling value prop than "Neat apparel" for a sweat-resistant shirt brand.

**Case study:** GrowthHit acquired Neat, a sweat-proof apparel brand on life support at $9K/month. The old store simply said "Neat." They rewrote the value proposition to "Unlike regular shirts, ours hide sweat. Make the switch," re-merchandised around bundles, and rebuilt the site for cold traffic. The brand grew to six figures per month.

### mom-test

**Question:** What questions would still give you useful answers if you asked your mother about your product?

**Explanation:** Mom Test interviewing is a style of customer conversation that asks about past behavior, concrete facts, and current workarounds instead of hypotheticals, opinions, or leading questions. The goal is to make lying or polite agreement impossible.

**Apply:** Replace "Would you use X?" with "Tell me about the last time this happened. What did you do?" across your next 10 customer calls.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders ask friends what they think and get polite lies; the Mom Test turns those conversations into evidence that does not fall apart after launch.

**Common misconception:** The Mom Test means asking family for feedback. It means asking questions that even your biased mother cannot answer falsely.

**Example:** Instead of "Would an auto-status report be useful?", ask "Walk me through how you prepared last week's status report. How long did it take? What went wrong?"

**Case study:** A founder building an ops status-reporting tool asked "Would this be useful?" in early interviews, got positive answers, and built it. Pilot usage died after week four. Retrospecting with the Mom Test, he realized the interviews had never asked about the actual workflow, costs, or existing habits.

### earlyvangelist

**Question:** Who loves your unfinished product enough to shape it with you?

**Explanation:** Earlyvangelists are visionary customers who have the problem, recognize it, are already building or paying for partial solutions, and are willing to take a risk on an unfinished product because they can see the potential. They are not just early adopters; they are co-creators and champions.

**Apply:** Build a 4-point screen (has the problem, knows they have it, is trying to solve it, will pay/tell others) and only sell to people who pass all four.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders waste time selling to lukewarm users; earlyvangelists pay, refer, and co-create, giving you the signal and revenue to keep building.

**Common misconception:** Earlyvangelists are beta testers who use your product for free. Real earlyvangelists feel enough pain that they will pay and evangelize, even when the product is rough.

**Example:** The one IT leader in a founder's pitch who not only buys the vision but demands the product be installed in his company before a feature list exists.

**Case study:** Steve Blank describes the Epiphany case where a former student could only get one of ten prospects excited about the minimum feature set, but that one person "couldn't wait to help get our product into his company." This earlyvangelist became the guide for features and roadmap.

### riskiest-assumption

**Question:** If only one of your beliefs about this idea could be wrong, which one would kill it?

**Explanation:** Your riskiest assumption is the leap of faith with the highest potential impact on your idea and the least supporting evidence. Testing it first protects you from the most expensive mistake.

**Apply:** Run an Assumption Mapping session, rank assumptions by importance and evidence, then design the cheapest 48-hour test for the top-right one.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders jump into the build because coding is fun; the riskiest assumption forces you to test whether anyone cares before you sink months into engineering.

**Common misconception:** The riskiest thing is whether we can build it. Usually the riskiest assumption is whether anyone cares, not whether you can ship it.

**Example:** Before building the full product, an AI maintenance assistant team manually generated recommendations behind a prototype to see if supervisors would act on them.

**Case study:** Buffer's Joel Gascoigne ran a two-page landing-page smoke test ("Plans and Pricing") before writing product code. He tested whether users would click through and provide an email, measuring willingness to pay before building the scheduler.

### minimum-viable-product

**Question:** What is the smallest thing you can put in front of customers that still teaches you something real?

**Explanation:** A minimum viable product is the version of a new product that lets a team collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort. It is not a rough prototype built for its own sake; it is a deliberate experiment designed to test a specific leap-of-faith assumption. The right MVP is judged by what it can disprove, not by how many features it includes.

**Apply:** State the one riskiest assumption you want to test, then build only the smallest artifact—landing page, manual service, or working slice—that can confirm or refute it in under two weeks.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders can ship a full app in a weekend, but shipping the wrong full app wastes months; an MVP forces you to learn whether anyone cares before you scale the build.

**Common misconception:** An MVP is a half-finished product you rush out to get users. It is a learning tool scoped to a specific assumption, and it should deliver enough value that the test produces honest behavior.

**Example:** Instead of building a full meal-planning app, the Food on the Table founders manually created grocery lists and recipes for one paying customer to learn what they actually valued.

**Case study:** IMVU's original MVP took months, but it validated that users wanted 3D avatars before the team built the full virtual-world platform; Eric Ries later used the term to describe any smallest experiment that maximizes validated learning.

### concierge-mvp

**Question:** Can you deliver the value of your product by hand before you build the software?

**Explanation:** A concierge MVP delivers the promised customer outcome manually, in person or via simple tools, while the customer knows a human is doing the work. It replaces automation with white-glove service so you can test whether anyone wants the result, what the real workflow looks like, and how much they will pay. Because there is no code to maintain, you can iterate the experience in hours rather than sprints.

**Apply:** Find one early adopter, sell them the outcome at a real price, then perform every step manually for 2-4 weeks while documenting what they love, what they skip, and what breaks.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders want to start with a scalable app; a concierge MVP lets you validate demand and learn the exact feature set before you automate anything.

**Common misconception:** A concierge MVP is a service business you plan to keep running. It is a temporary learning tool; once you know what customers value, you replace the human steps with software.

**Example:** A grocery-list app founder visits a customer's home each week, asks what her family likes, checks store sales, and hands her a printed meal plan and shopping list for $9.95.

**Case study:** Food on the Table used a concierge MVP: the CEO and VP of product manually planned meals for their first paying customer each week, collecting checks and feedback before writing the platform that eventually scaled nationwide.

### fake-door-test

**Question:** What if you could measure real demand for a feature before building it?

**Explanation:** A fake-door test presents a realistic entry point—a button, menu item, or landing page—for a product or feature that does not yet exist, then tracks who tries to use it. Clickers land on an honest 'coming soon' or waitlist page, and the click rate becomes a demand signal. It is one of the cheapest ways to validate interest using real behavior rather than survey answers.

**Apply:** Add a believable 'Export to PDF' button or nav item in your product, route clickers to a transparent waitlist, set a pass/fail click-rate threshold before the test, and compare the result to real traffic.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders can instrument a fake element in a day; it tells you whether a feature is worth months of engineering before you write the first real API.

**Common misconception:** A fake door is a trick that annoys users. Done honestly—with a clear 'coming soon' message and no charges—it is a standard pretotyping method that respects users while preventing wasted builds.

**Example:** A team debates adding an AI summarizer; they add a 'Summarize with AI' button to the editor and find only 0.3% of active users click it, so they deprioritize.

**Case study:** Pretotyping methods popularized at Google X and Stanford use fake doors and painted-door tests to kill weak ideas fast; teams at Google tested product concepts with fake entry points before investing in full prototypes.

**Resources:** https://amplitude.com/explore/experiment/fake-door-testing, https://www.exponentially.com/fake-door-test

### landing-page-experiment

**Question:** Can a single page tell you whether anyone will pay for something that does not exist yet?

**Explanation:** A landing-page experiment is a low-fidelity demand test that drives real traffic to a page describing a product or value proposition and measures signups, pre-orders, or clicks. It separates genuine interest from polite encouragement.

**Apply:** Build a one-page offer with a headline, 3 bullets, and a single CTA (waitlist / pre-order / "See plans and pricing"). Spend $200-500 on targeted ads and track conversion to the CTA.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders can spin up a landing page before a single API call; it measures real demand so you do not write code for an audience that will not convert.

**Common misconception:** Lots of page views means validation. Only the conversion to a commitment (email, waitlist, payment) counts; traffic is cheap.

**Example:** A founder tests two headlines—"A/B testing for modern websites" vs. "Start winning more customers, without more traffic"—and sees a 4x lift in signups from the outcome-driven one.

**Case study:** Evil Martians validated Thicket, a live humanities-learning marketplace, with a $2K ad budget. They generated 36K impressions, 128 cold waitlist signups, and identified a landing-page variant at 95% confidence. The experiment confirmed student demand before the full marketplace was built.

### qualitative-vs-quantitative-research

**Question:** When should you interview five people and when should you survey five hundred?

**Explanation:** Qualitative research answers why and how through deep, small-sample methods like interviews and usability tests. Quantitative research answers how many and how often through large-sample, structured methods like surveys, analytics, and A/B tests.

**Apply:** Use qual to explore unknowns and generate hypotheses, use quant to size or validate them, and combine both when the cost of being wrong is high.

**Why it matters:** Relying only on gut or dashboard numbers is a trap for vibe coders; combining both types of research prevents building a product that feels right in your head but fails in real life.

**Common misconception:** More survey responses always beat a few interviews. Small-sample qualitative research often reveals the 'why' that massive surveys can only confirm, not discover.

**Example:** A product team runs 8 user interviews to surface why users abandon onboarding, then runs a 500-person survey to rank the top reasons across segments.

**Case study:** Toptal used in-depth interviews plus a MaxDiff survey to shape the launch of Managed Delivery; the mixed-method research refined value propositions, informed landing-page messaging, and helped close significant contracts.

**Resources:** https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/, https://www.researchbyjulia.com/toptal-product-launch-case-study

### usability-testing

**Question:** How do you find the problems in your design before you ship them to thousands of users?

**Explanation:** Usability testing is an observational method in which representative users perform realistic tasks while a researcher watches and listens, uncovering problems and opportunities in the design.

**Apply:** Run 5-user moderated tests on a prototype or live product, focus on critical user tasks, and iterate between rounds instead of running one large study.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders ship fast, but confusing flows kill adoption; catching usability issues early prevents polished code from being rejected because users cannot figure it out.

**Common misconception:** Usability testing needs dozens of users and a lab. Five targeted users will usually surface the majority of critical issues in a single afternoon.

**Example:** Mozilla tested paper prototypes of its support site with Firefox users, iterated seven versions in two weeks, and then built the HTML version.

**Case study:** Mozilla's support-site redesign used iterative usability testing and data mining to achieve a 233% improvement in findability and a 70% reduction in support calls.

### survey-design

**Question:** How do you ask questions that produce reliable answers instead of biased noise?

**Explanation:** Survey design is the craft of writing clear, neutral, answerable questions that measure what you intend without leading respondents, overloading them, or mixing two questions into one.

**Apply:** Define one research goal, write closed-ended questions, avoid double-barreled and leading wording, pre-test with 5 users, and keep the survey short.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders throw together quick polls; bad questions produce flattering but fake data, so careful survey design is the difference between learning and fooling yourself.

**Common misconception:** A survey is just a list of questions. Biased wording, leading order, and double-barreled questions can make the results worse than no data at all.

**Example:** Instead of 'How easy was it to use our amazing new checkout?' ask 'How would you rate the checkout experience?' with a balanced scale.

**Case study:** Crayola used Qualtrics to test the 'Colors of the World' product line name and packaging with over 9,000 parents and teachers, confirming strong purchase intent before launch.

### customer-journey-map

### product-analytics

**Question:** How do you know what users actually do inside your product once they sign up?

**Explanation:** Product analytics is the practice of capturing and analyzing user behavior inside a digital product at the event level. Instead of counting page views, you track individual actions—signups, invites, purchases, feature use—and segment them by user properties and cohorts. A clear tracking plan and north-star metric turn raw events into decisions about what to build next.

**Apply:** Write a tracking plan with 15-30 named events, instrument signup, activation, and key feature actions, then build one dashboard that shows weekly active users, activation rate, and retention by cohort.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders can launch without analytics, but flying blind means you optimize the wrong flows; product analytics shows which features drive value and which ones sit unused.

**Common misconception:** More events and dashboards equal better insights. Tracking too much creates noise; the best analytics start with the questions you want to answer, then add only the events that answer them.

**Example:** A SaaS team tracks 'project created,' 'teammate invited,' and 'report exported' to discover that users who invite a teammate in the first week retain 4x longer.

**Case study:** Amplitude's product benchmark study of 10,600+ digital products found that the top 10% retain 4x more users at month one than the median, and early activation is the strongest predictor of long-term retention.

### funnel-analysis

**Question:** Where are users dropping out of the path to value?

**Explanation:** Funnel analysis maps the sequence of actions users take toward a meaningful outcome and measures the drop-off between each step. It turns aggregate activity into a diagnostic view of where momentum slows or stops inside the product. The goal is not the overall conversion rate; it is finding the one or two leakiest steps you can fix for the biggest impact.

**Apply:** Define one user goal, list the 3-5 key events leading to it, calculate the completion rate between each step, then segment by traffic source or user role to find the biggest drop-off.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders watch top-line numbers and miss the leak; funnel analysis shows exactly which step is killing activation, adoption, or upgrade so you stop guessing.

**Common misconception:** A bigger top-of-funnel fixes a broken funnel. Pouring more users into a leaky funnel just wastes acquisition spend; the highest-ROI move is fixing the biggest drop-off.

**Example:** A checkout funnel shows 40% of users reach the cart but only 8% complete payment; the payment step becomes the first optimization target.

**Case study:** VWO clients use funnel analysis to identify that most drop-offs happen at single points such as a confusing form or hidden CTA, and targeted experiments at those points often lift conversion by 20-40%.

**Resources:** https://vwo.com/blog/funnel-analysis/, https://plane.so/blog/conversion-funnel-analysis-for-product-managers-how-to-spot-drop-offs

### feedback-loop

**Common misconception:** Collecting feedback is the same as acting on it. Without a tight triage and decision cadence, feedback becomes noise that paralyzes the team.

### customer-feedback-triage

**Question:** How do you turn a flood of feedback into a small set of actionable opportunities?

**Explanation:** Customer feedback triage is the upstream process of collecting, tagging, de-duplicating, and routing feedback from support tickets, sales notes, surveys, and interviews before it reaches prioritization. It separates signal from noise by clustering requests into problem themes and attaching source, segment, and frequency. Without triage, the loudest request wins instead of the most important one.

**Apply:** Create one intake channel, tag every item with source, theme, sentiment, and customer segment, run a weekly 30-minute triage session to cluster themes, and only pass scored opportunities to the prioritization meeting.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders collect feedback in Slack and inboxes and then build whatever was mentioned last; triage turns scattered notes into a structured backlog tied to real customer problems.

**Common misconception:** Triage is just sorting feature requests into a ranked list. Real triage reframes requests as customer opportunities, removes duplicates, and closes the loop so users keep giving signal.

**Example:** Twenty support tickets, ten sales notes, and three NPS comments all mention 'export problems'; triage clusters them into one opportunity—'users need to move data into other tools'—and proposes a single integration solution.

**Case study:** Product teams using weekly extraction passes with support tickets have turned support queues into the richest discovery input, finding that a few recurring themes often explain most customer pain.

### opportunity-solution-tree

**Question:** How do you show which customer problems connect to which business outcomes and which possible solutions?

**Explanation:** The opportunity-solution tree (Teresa Torres) is a visual map with the desired outcome at the top, opportunities (user problems) as branches, and solutions/experiments as leaves.

**Apply:** Pick one outcome, list the customer opportunities that affect it, brainstorm solutions for the highest-priority opportunities, and design experiments to test the best ones.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders jump from cool idea to cool idea; an opportunity-solution tree forces every idea to connect to a customer problem and a business outcome so you stop solving for yourself.

**Common misconception:** It is just another prioritization template. The real value is separating outcomes, opportunities, and solutions so you stop debating solutions before you agree on the goal.

**Example:** For a Netflix goal of 'increase monthly viewing time,' an opportunity is 'users struggle to decide what to watch'; one solution is an improved recommendation row validated by an A/B test.

**Case study:** SuperAwesome introduced opportunity-solution trees across teams to turn an unstructured backlog of ideas into a visible map of how each solution connected to a customer opportunity and an OKR.

### now-next-later

**Question:** How do you communicate direction without committing to fake delivery dates?

**Explanation:** A now-next-later roadmap organizes work into three time horizons: Now (committed and in progress), Next (validated and ready to start), and Later (big bets still being explored).

**Apply:** Replace timeline deadlines with three theme-based columns, review them monthly, and make the criteria for moving an item between columns explicit.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders overpromise dates to stay exciting; now-next-later lets you communicate commitment without lying about a six-month plan you cannot keep.

**Common misconception:** It is just a three-column to-do list. Without clear criteria for moving items across columns, it becomes another vague wishlist.

**Example:** Now = role-based admin and audit controls; Next = workflow analytics; Later = AI-powered features.

**Case study:** ProdPad's Janna Bastow invented the format after constant date slippage. A $500M B2B workflow software company used it to expose that many 'urgent' sales requests were one-offs that did not support the retention strategy.

### okrs

**Question:** How do you set goals that are ambitious enough to stretch the team but measurable enough to grade?

**Explanation:** Objectives and Key Results (OKRs) are a goal-setting framework where objectives are qualitative and ambitious, and key results are measurable outcomes. 60–70% achievement is typically considered success.

**Apply:** Set 3–5 objectives per quarter, each with 3–5 measurable key results, make them public, and separate OKR grading from performance reviews.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders celebrate shipping features, even if nothing changes; OKRs keep a small team focused on measurable user behavior instead of checkmarks.

**Common misconception:** Hitting 100% of OKRs means success. The best OKRs are designed to be ambitious, and 60–70% achievement often means the targets were meaningful.

**Example:** Sundar Pichai's Chrome OKR in 2008 focused on growing weekly active users through measurable weekly targets.

**Case study:** Google adopted OKRs in 1999 when it had about 40 employees and has scaled the practice to over 200,000 people, using it to align small teams on industry-changing bets such as Search, Chrome, and Android.

### outcome-vs-output

**Question:** Are you shipping features or changing user behavior?

**Explanation:** Outputs are what the team builds; outcomes are the measurable changes in user behavior or business results that the outputs create. Product teams should be measured by outcomes.

**Apply:** For the next initiative, define the outcome in the form of a behavior change or metric, then let the team decide the best output to deliver it.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders ship fast and count releases; focusing on outcomes forces you to ask whether user behavior actually changed, not just whether the code merged.

**Common misconception:** More output means more progress. A team shipping fewer things that change user behavior is outperforming one that ships features no one uses.

**Example:** Instead of 'ship five onboarding tooltips' (output), set 'increase onboarding completion from 28% to 40%' (outcome).

**Case study:** A SaaS onboarding redesign moved from output thinking to outcome thinking and raised activation by 42% by reducing steps, adding a one-minute setup path, and using contextual nudges.

### leading-lagging-indicators

**Question:** How can you tell whether you are on track to hit next quarter's goals before it is too late to act?

**Explanation:** Lagging indicators measure results after the fact (revenue, churn, retention). Leading indicators are earlier signals that predict those results and can still be influenced.

**Apply:** Identify one lagging outcome you care about, find the earliest user behavior that statistically predicts it, and track that as your leading indicator.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders wait for revenue or churn to know they are in trouble; leading indicators give you an early warning while you can still fix the product.

**Common misconception:** Lagging indicators are bad. You need both: lagging confirms results, and leading tells you whether the result is still achievable.

**Example:** A B2B SaaS team found that users who invite a second team member within 7 days renew at 74% versus 31% for those who do not, making that invite a leading indicator of 6-month retention.

**Case study:** A 30–50 person e-commerce SaaS company used activation as a leading indicator and identified $2.5M in at-risk revenue after fixing an invisible onboarding drop-off.

### feature-prioritization

**Question:** How do you decide which feature is worth building when every stakeholder thinks theirs is the most important?

**Explanation:** Feature prioritization is the process of ranking candidate features by value, effort, risk, and strategic fit so that limited engineering time is spent on the highest-impact work instead of the loudest request.

**Apply:** Collect evidence for the top 10–15 items in your backlog, pick one framework, score each idea, then present a ranked list with the scoring logic visible.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders build what the loudest user asks for; prioritization forces value, effort, and risk onto the table so you do not drown in low-impact work.

**Common misconception:** Prioritization is about saying yes to the best ideas. It is mostly about saying no to good ideas that do not fit this quarter's goals.

**Example:** A B2B SaaS team plots SSO/SAML (high ARR at stake, medium effort) above a mobile app (low ARR at stake, high effort) after weighting revenue-at-risk and implementation cost.

**Case study:** CloudSync, a B2B project management tool, analyzed six customer call transcripts plus account data. SSO/SAML represented $513K ARR at risk while the mobile app represented only $73K, which flipped the prioritization away from the most-mentioned request and toward the highest-value one.

### value-vs-effort

**Question:** What is the fastest way to separate quick wins from expensive bets?

**Explanation:** Value vs. effort is a 2×2 prioritization matrix that maps each initiative's expected value against the effort required, producing quick wins, major projects, fill-ins, and thankless tasks.

**Apply:** Score every candidate 1–10 on value and 1–10 on effort, place them on the matrix, and schedule the top-left quadrant before the bottom-right one.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders take on big bets because they look heroic; value vs. effort keeps a small team focused on quick wins that build momentum instead of risky moonshots.

**Common misconception:** High-value, high-effort projects are always the best bet. A series of quick wins often builds momentum faster than one giant project that may never ship.

**Example:** 'Add CSV export' is a quick win (high value, low effort); 'rebuild the analytics engine' is a major project (high value, high effort).

**Case study:** Thundra implemented value vs. effort in Fibery, adding Value, Effort, and Score fields so the team stopped re-litigating priorities each sprint and planning meetings became shorter and more focused.

### rice-framework

**Question:** How can you compare very different ideas with a single number without hiding the uncertainty in the estimate?

**Explanation:** RICE is a prioritization formula—Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort—created by Sean McBride at Intercom to make roadmap debates more objective and to surface how confident the team really is.

**Apply:** For every candidate feature, estimate the four RICE factors and use the resulting score as the starting point for discussion, not the final answer.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders debate roadmap by gut; RICE makes the trade-offs explicit—reach, impact, confidence, and effort—so decisions are grounded, not political.

**Common misconception:** RICE gives the 'right' answer. The score is a starting point for discussion, not a replacement for judgment.

**Example:** A growth team scores 'client comment threads' at 240, 'reporting dashboard' at 180, and 'onboarding wizard' at 200, then picks the comment threads first.

**Case study:** Intercom's growth team built RICE to compare conversion-focused projects against a single goal. Adding 'Reach' as a multiplier revealed that earlier frameworks were underweighting audience size and letting pet projects win.

### kano-model

**Question:** How do you know which features will delight users and which will just prevent complaints?

**Explanation:** The Kano model classifies features by how satisfaction changes as functionality changes: must-be (expected), one-dimensional (more is better), attractive (delight when present, not missed when absent), indifferent, and reverse.

**Apply:** Run a Kano survey with functional and dysfunctional questions for 8–12 candidate features, plot the results, and use the categories to sequence work.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders think more features equal more love; Kano shows which features are expected, which delight, and which nobody cares about, so you spend effort where it matters.

**Common misconception:** Every feature should aim to delight. Must-be features will never wow users, but their absence will frustrate them and kill adoption.

**Example:** In a chat app, message history is a must-be, 'unsend' is attractive, and a custom cursor color is indifferent.

**Case study:** Osome ran a Kano survey to prioritize 40 chat improvements. The work revealed customers were using chat as document cloud storage, which reframed the product strategy and produced a satisfaction-driven backlog.

**Resources:** https://www.nngroup.com/articles/prioritization-methods/, https://annakhakho.substack.com/p/case-study-researching-the-value

### product-market-fit-signals

**Common misconception:** A few paying customers or good press means you have product-market fit.

**Example:** A team messaging app sees signups plateau but messages sent per team and NPS keep climbing; that is a fit signal even if revenue is modest.

### the-40-percent-rule

**Question:** How disappointed would your users have to be before you can confidently say you have product-market fit?

**Explanation:** The 40% rule, from Sean Ellis's research, states that if at least 40% of surveyed active users would be very disappointed if they could no longer use the product, you have initial product-market fit. Below 40%, growth usually stalls regardless of marketing spend.

**Apply:** Survey 40+ active users who used the product at least twice in the last two weeks with the question, 'How would you feel if you could no longer use this product?' and calculate the 'very disappointed' share.

**Why it matters:** It is a leading, benchmarked indicator of sustainable growth, not a feeling.

**Common misconception:** You need thousands of responses; a few dozen active users give directionally valid results.

**Example:** A premium email client scores 22% very disappointed—below the threshold—so the team knows not to pour money into ads yet.

**Case study:** Superhuman initially scored only 22% on the Sean Ellis question. After segmenting high-expectation customers and building a roadmap to double down on what fans loved while removing blockers, the score rose to 58% and growth followed.

### superhuman-pmf-survey

**Question:** How can a four-question survey become your product roadmap?

**Explanation:** The Superhuman PMF survey adapts the Sean Ellis question by adding three open-ended prompts: who would benefit most, the main benefit received, and how to improve. It turns a single score into segmentation, ideal customer profile, and a prioritized roadmap.

**Apply:** Send the four-question survey to your most active users, then split responses into 'very disappointed' (double down), 'somewhat disappointed' (remove blockers), and 'not disappointed' (ignore).

**Why it matters:** Aggregate PMF scores hide the truth; the survey's real power is segmenting by enthusiasm.

**Common misconception:** The goal is to maximize the score by any means; the real goal is to find the narrow audience that already loves you.

**Example:** Users who are very disappointed report 'speed' as the main benefit, so the team prioritizes keyboard shortcuts and stops building a mobile app for now.

**Case study:** Rahul Vohra's Superhuman used a four-question Typeform survey. Segmenting down to the most disappointed users raised the apparent PMF score by 10 points immediately and guided a roadmap that ultimately reached 58% very disappointed.

### net-promoter-score

**Question:** If someone asked your users whether they would recommend you, would the answers predict growth?

**Explanation:** Net Promoter Score asks users how likely they are to recommend the product on a 0-10 scale. Promoters (9-10) are strong word-of-mouth drivers, passives (7-8) are at risk, and detractors (0-6) can churn and damage reputation.

**Apply:** Run an in-app NPS survey tied to a real user milestone, segment the open-ended feedback by role, and assign detractor themes to the team that can fix them.

**Why it matters:** NPS correlates with retention and organic acquisition; a falling NPS is an early churn warning.

**Common misconception:** A high NPS alone means you are growing; it must be paired with behavioral retention and revenue.

**Example:** A fintech app sees promoters using a feature 3x more than detractors, so it redesigns onboarding to expose that feature earlier.

**Case study:** Zipcar's CPO rebuilt member-obsession practices and in-context feedback, improving NPS from 36 to 56 (+50%) by fixing car cleanliness, damage, and maintenance issues, which also reduced asset depreciation costs.

### churn

**Question:** What if the users leaving are not the wrong fit, but the product is failing them at a predictable moment?

**Explanation:** Churn is the rate at which users or revenue stop using or paying over a period. It can be voluntary (cancellation) or involuntary (failed payment), and it often clusters around specific product friction points.

**Why it matters:** Churn is the direct cost of weak activation and retention; reducing it often has higher ROI than new acquisition.

**Common misconception:** Churn is a customer-success problem only; often the root cause is onboarding or a missing feature.

**Example answer:** Churn is the rate at which customers or recurring revenue leave a business over a period of time.

**Example:** A SaaS sees 70% of churn happen in the first 14 days; redesigning the empty state in that window cuts it in half.

### retention-curve

**Question:** How does your user base actually decay after the first day, and where does it flatten?

**Explanation:** A retention curve plots the share of a user cohort that returns on each day or week after their first use. The shape reveals whether users are habituating or leaking; a curve that flattens to a non-zero baseline indicates a durable core.

**Apply:** Build a daily or weekly retention curve for your last four sign-up cohorts and mark the day the curve stops dropping—this is your stabilization point.

**Why it matters:** Flat retention is a stronger signal of product-market fit than top-of-funnel growth.

**Common misconception:** A high Day-1 retention guarantees long-term success; what matters is where the curve flattens.

**Example:** A meditation app has 45% D1 but 5% D30; another starts at 30% and stays at 12%—the flatter curve wins.

**Case study:** Duolingo tracks New User Retention Rate and Current User Retention Rate as part of a Markov model, finding that improving retention by just 1% compounds into significant DAU growth and that streaks are critical for flattening early retention.

### cohort-retention

**Question:** Are you measuring true retention, or just adding new users on top of old churn?

**Explanation:** Cohort retention groups users by the week or month they started and tracks how many return over time. It separates new-user acquisition effects from the actual staying power of the product.

**Apply:** Create a cohort retention table for the last 12 weekly sign-up cohorts and compare each cell to find a consistent drop-off week.

**Why it matters:** Aggregate retention can be inflated by growth; cohorts reveal whether each vintage of users is getting better or worse.

**Common misconception:** Monthly active users growing means retention is healthy; MAU can hide high churn behind new signups.

**Example:** A subscription brand's March cohort retains 30% at month six while the June cohort retains only 12%, showing a product or onboarding regression.

**Case study:** PlantPaper's early COVID cohorts showed 95% still subscribing at six months and 90% at twelve months, but the founder realized low prices made that excellent retention unprofitable—proving the need to pair retention with unit economics.

### d1-d7-d30-retention

### pirate-metrics

**Question:** Are you measuring the whole user lifecycle, or only the parts that make you feel good?

**Common misconception:** AARRR is a growth strategy; it is a measurement framework, and the strategy comes from diagnosing the bottleneck.

### activation-funnel

### progressive-onboarding

**Question:** Do you really need to ask for everything up front, or can you request it exactly when it becomes useful?

**Explanation:** Progressive onboarding reveals features, permissions, and setup steps only when the user needs them. It reduces cognitive load and keeps first-time friction low while still teaching advanced behavior later.

**Apply:** Audit your signup flow and defer every question that is not needed for the user's first five minutes of value.

**Why it matters:** Cognitive load in the first session is the main cause of early drop-off; progressive disclosure keeps users moving.

**Common misconception:** Collecting all information early makes onboarding faster; it usually kills conversion before value.

**Example:** A payment platform asks for business details only when the user is ready to go live, not at account creation.

**Case study:** Stripe treats developer onboarding as a product surface and uses progressive disclosure to ask for information only when it is needed, which has helped it onboard millions of merchants and process over $1 trillion in payment volume.

### activation-rate

**Question:** Of the people who sign up, how many actually do the thing that makes them want to stay?

**Explanation:** Activation rate is the percentage of new users who complete the critical action that indicates they have experienced the product's core value. It is the bridge between acquisition and retention.

**Apply:** Define one activation event for each user role and set a weekly activation-rate target for the last 100 signups.

**Why it matters:** High acquisition with low activation is just expensive churn.

**Common misconception:** A signup is an active user; activation only counts when the user reaches value.

**Example:** A heatmap tool counts activation as a user who uploads a screenshot and creates a heatmap analysis.

**Case study:** Blip's chatbot builder had a 28.45% activation rate. Using Appcues to guide users through create-bot, test-bot, and publish-bot steps, funnel completion rose to 63.74%—a 124% increase in activation—and time-to-value fell from 66 minutes to 6.8 minutes.

### engagement-loop

**Question:** What makes a user want to open your product again tomorrow without you having to remind them?

**Explanation:** An engagement loop is a self-reinforcing cycle of trigger, action, and reward that keeps users returning. Strong loops create habits and reduce dependency on notifications or paid re-engagement.

**Apply:** Map the trigger, action, and reward for your most retained users and redesign the product to tighten the cycle.

**Why it matters:** Sustainable retention comes from internal triggers, not from marketing spend.

**Common misconception:** Push notifications drive retention; they re-engage, but they do not create the habit loop.

**Example:** A learning app: push reminder (trigger) -> complete a quick lesson (action) -> streak + XP (reward) -> pride (internal trigger).

**Case study:** Duolingo's streak feature, backed by 600+ experiments, creates a daily habit loop. Users who maintain a 7-day streak are 240% more likely to become long-term users, and the company reached 56.5 million DAUs by Q1 2026.

### product-qualified-lead

**Question:** How do you know a free user is ready to buy without asking them?

**Apply:** Identify the 2-3 in-product actions that best predict paid conversion and create a PQL score that triggers sales outreach.

**Why it matters:** PQLs convert better and faster because they have already experienced value.

**Common misconception:** A PQL is anyone who signs up; PQLs are defined by behavior, not registration.

**Example:** A collaboration tool scores a PQL when a free user creates three documents, invites two teammates, and returns twice in seven days.

### beta-program

### product-launch

**Question:** What makes a product launch more than a single announcement post?

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders often push to main and hope users show up; a structured launch turns a release into a distribution event with owned channels, social proof, and clear metrics.

**Common misconception:** A launch is the day you go public. The launch is a campaign that starts weeks before release and continues for days after; most durable growth happens in the follow-through, not the announcement.

**Example:** A design tool launches with a clear 'hello world' post, a demo video, and a two-week plan to respond to every comment and collect testimonials.

### experiment-design

**Question:** What is the written contract that makes an A/B test answer the right business question?

**Explanation:** Experiment design is the pre-launch specification of the hypothesis, randomization unit, control, treatment, primary/secondary metrics, minimum detectable effect, sample size, and stopping rules. Locking these before launch prevents p-hacking and invalid comparisons.

**Apply:** Write a one-page experiment design doc before building the variant.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders A/B test random changes; a written experiment design makes the result trustworthy and the learning reusable, instead of a coin flip.

**Common misconception:** An A/B test is just trying two versions. Without a control, randomization, and pre-registered metrics, you are not learning; you are hoping.

**Example:** A checkout-button test defines the population as signed-in shoppers, the primary metric as orders completed within 7 days, and a 15% relative MDE.

**Case study:** ShopWise UK product-page redesign pre-registered a 50/50 user-level split, conversion rate as the primary metric, a 15% relative MDE, 22,605 users per group, and a 14-day run covering two full weekly cycles; the test reached 96.5% power with 48,312 total users.

**Resources:** https://support.optimizely.com/hc/en-us/articles/4410282997005-Create-a-basic-experiment-plan, https://optipilot.com/strategy/experiment-design-ab-testing

### statistical-significance

**Question:** How unusual would your results be if the change actually had no effect?

**Explanation:** Statistical significance measures the probability that an observed difference is due to random chance. A high threshold (e.g., p < 0.05 or 95% confidence) means the effect is unlikely to be noise.

**Apply:** Set the alpha threshold and minimum sample before the test; do not call a winner early.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders ship a winning variant too early and call it a success; significance tells you whether the result is real or just lucky.

**Common misconception:** Statistical significance means the effect is large or important. It only means the difference is unlikely to be noise; you still need practical significance.

**Example:** A conversion rate of 3.30% vs. 3.20% with p = 0.007 means only a 0.7% chance the lift is due to random variation.

**Case study:** HappyRobot A/B tested a Text-to-Speech voice for a telecom upsell campaign; Voice B lifted upgrade acceptance from 13.01% to 15.68%, a 20.1% relative improvement that was statistically significant over three weeks.

**Resources:** https://support.optimizely.com/hc/en-us/articles/4410284003341-Statistical-significance, https://www.statsig.com/perspectives/treatment-vs-control-ab-test-guide

### control-group

**Question:** What does "normal" look like while you test a change?

**Explanation:** The control group is the current experience held constant against the treatment. Random assignment makes the groups equivalent except for the one change, so you can attribute differences to the treatment.

**Apply:** Hold out a randomly assigned control in every experiment; never compare post-launch numbers to a pre-launch baseline.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders compare this week to last week and call it a win; a control group lets you know if the improvement came from your change or from seasonal traffic.

**Common misconception:** A control group is unnecessary if you already have a baseline. Without a concurrent control, seasonal effects, traffic changes, and luck can fake a win.

**Example:** 50% of shoppers see the current checkout (control) and 50% see the redesigned checkout (treatment).

**Case study:** CanvasCore ran a 45-day 50/50 A/B test on its product page; the control converted at 2.1%, the redesign at 2.58%, a 23% lift with 99.2% confidence across 18,400 visitors per variation.

**Resources:** https://docs.statsig.com/guides/abn-tests, https://www.statsig.com/perspectives/treatment-vs-control-ab-test-guide

### minimum-detectable-effect

**Question:** What is the smallest lift your experiment is powered to detect?

**Explanation:** MDE is the smallest relative or absolute improvement an experiment can reliably distinguish from noise given the baseline rate, significance, and power. A smaller MDE requires proportionally more traffic.

**Apply:** Choose the MDE based on the smallest business-meaningful lift and compute sample size from it before launch.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders run experiments that are too small to learn from; setting the MDE tells you the smallest meaningful improvement and the traffic you need to detect it.

**Common misconception:** A smaller MDE is always better. Chasing tiny effects inflates sample size and can trap you in long tests that miss the forest for the trees.

**Example:** A 5% baseline with a 10% relative MDE means the test is designed to detect a variant rate of 5.5% or higher.

**Case study:** DRIP analyzed thousands of e-commerce A/B tests and found mean winning uplifts of only ~2.9% on conversion and ~4.2% on revenue per visitor; teams that set MDE at 5%+ miss most real improvements.

**Resources:** https://support.optimizely.com/hc/en-us/articles/4410283338253-Use-minimum-detectable-effect-to-prioritize-experiments, https://atticusli.com/blog/posts/minimum-detectable-effect-ab-testing/

### sample-size

**Question:** How many users do you need before you can trust the result?

**Explanation:** Sample size is the number of observations per variant required to detect the MDE at the chosen significance and power. Too small yields false positives/negatives; too large wastes time and traffic.

**Apply:** Use a sample-size calculator with baseline rate, MDE, alpha, power, and number of variants before launching.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders call a test the moment a variant looks good; a proper sample size protects you from shipping a false winner and wasting engineering work.

**Common misconception:** More users always make an experiment better. There is an optimal sample for each effect size and power; running too long can waste time and money on trivial differences.

**Example:** To detect a 10% relative lift on a 3% baseline at 95% significance and 80% power, you need roughly 26,000 visitors per variant.

**Case study:** ShopWise UK's checkout-funnel A/B test had a 3.20% baseline and a 15% relative MDE; the power analysis required 22,605 users per group and the final test reached 24,156 per group, giving 96.5% power.

**Resources:** https://support.optimizely.com/hc/en-us/articles/4410283969165-How-long-to-run-an-experiment, https://optipilot.com/tools/sample-size-calculator

### multivariate-testing

**Question:** How do you know which combination of changes is best?

**Explanation:** Multivariate testing varies multiple elements simultaneously to find the winning combination and reveal interaction effects. It needs more traffic than a simple A/B test.

**Apply:** Use MVT only on high-traffic pages; keep the number of combinations low enough to reach adequate sample per cell.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders change many things at once and wonder what worked; multivariate testing reveals which combination actually drives the lift, as long as you have the traffic.

**Common misconception:** MVT is just a faster way to run A/B tests. It multiplies the required traffic, and without enough users it will produce weak or false results.

**Example:** Testing 3 headlines × 2 images × 2 CTA colors creates 12 variants; a CTA color may only win with a specific headline.

**Case study:** BusinessSummaries.com ran an MVT on its subscription offer page across price, bonus offer, and six other variables; the winning combination included a higher price and lifted revenue 697%.

### willingness-to-pay

**Question:** What range of prices will customers actually accept?

**Explanation:** Willingness-to-pay research (e.g., Van Westendorp, conjoint) quantifies how much customers value a product and identifies price floors and ceilings before live testing.

**Apply:** Run a Van Westendorp or conjoint survey on your target segment before setting list prices or running live pricing tests.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders guess a price and leave money on the table; willingness-to-pay research finds the range customers actually value before you test live prices.

**Common misconception:** Customers will tell you the exact price they will pay. Direct pricing questions often produce misleading answers; structured methods like Van Westendorp or conjoint are more reliable.

**Example:** Four questions: "At what price is this too cheap to trust, a bargain, expensive but acceptable, too expensive?" Plot cumulative responses to find the acceptable range.

**Case study:** Survicate ran a 267-response willingness-to-pay survey and found different price sensitivity by use case and persona; segmenting pricing increased ARPU 27% with no churn increase.

### pricing-experiment

**Question:** How do you find the price that maximizes value without guessing?

**Explanation:** A pricing experiment varies price, packaging, or discounts to measure impact on conversion, revenue, and profit per visitor while holding other factors constant.

**Apply:** Run an A/B/n price test with a hold-out control and a fixed sample; measure profit per visitor, not just conversion.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders pick a price based on a competitor or gut; a pricing experiment shows what actually converts and what maximizes profit per visitor.

**Common misconception:** The winning price is the one with the highest conversion. Profit per visitor, lifetime value, and brand perception matter just as much as conversion rate.

**Example:** Test $9, $12, and $15 for the same feature set, each with a random user allocation.

**Case study:** Convertibles ran an A/B/n pricing test for an 8-figure adult wellness brand with two $99 products; raising Product A to $125 and lowering Product B to $90 lifted profit per visitor 54.7% (estimated +$40,573/month).

### network-effects

**Question:** Why does the product get better for every user who joins?

**Explanation:** Network effects mean value increases as more users join—direct (more people to connect with), indirect (more supply in a marketplace), or data-based (better recommendations). They create defensibility.

**Apply:** Identify which side of your product creates value for the other side, then prioritize acquiring the scarcer side.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders build features competitors can copy; network effects make your product more valuable as it grows, creating a moat that a well-funded clone cannot replicate overnight.

**Common misconception:** Network effects automatically make you a winner. You still have to solve the chicken-and-egg problem and reach critical mass before the effect matters.

**Example answer:** Network effects happen when a product becomes more valuable to each user as the total number of users grows.

**Example:** Each new host on Airbnb adds more inventory, which attracts more guests, which in turn attracts more hosts.

**Case study:** Airbnb grew from 1,000 users in 2009 to 1M by mid-2011; research on 2008–2013 data estimates a 1% increase in listings correlates with a 1.7% increase in potential users and vice versa.

### viral-loop

### growth-loop

**Question:** What is the self-reinforcing system that drives sustainable growth?

**Explanation:** A growth loop is a closed system where an input (users, content, money) produces an output that can be reinvested to generate more input. Loops compound; funnels leak.

**Apply:** Map one input-to-output loop for your product, measure cycle time and amplification, and remove friction at each step.

**Why it matters:** Vibe coders chase linear funnel improvements; a growth loop reinvests an output back into the input, compounding growth without scaling spend.

**Common misconception:** A growth loop is just a clever marketing campaign. It is a closed, product-driven system where the output creates more input, not a one-off tactic.

**Example:** A user uploads a file, shares it with a non-user, the non-user signs up, and the cycle repeats.

**Case study:** Notion rebuilt the product after a 2013 flop, then used collaboration, a template ecosystem, and user-generated content to create growth loops that reached 100M users with no paid ads by 2024.
